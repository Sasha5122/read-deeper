### A (dozat)
Marcus doesn’t just say “be patient.” He runs a deliberate practice that starts before contact, continues in the moment, and ends with either gentle correction or quiet forbearance. The core moves are:

1) Premeditate others’ faults each morning
- “Betimes in the morning say to thyself, This day I shall have to do with an idle… unthankful… envious… unsociable man… All these… through ignorance of that which is truly good and truly bad… he is my kinsman… we are all born to be fellow‑workers… What is it to chafe at, but to be in opposition [to nature]?” (p. 17)
- Purpose: inoculate yourself against surprise and anger; pre‑commit to cooperation and good will.

2) Remove the judgment that you’ve been harmed
- “Of things that are external, happen what will… as long as I conceive no such thing [as harm], I have no hurt; and it is in my power not to conceive any such thing.” (p. 59)
- “Doth any man offend? It is against himself that he doth offend: why should it trouble thee?” (p. 9)
- Purpose: the “injury” is your opinion; drop the label and the sting goes.

3) Inspect the offender’s mind; reframe as error, not malice
- “Whensoever any man doth trespass… consider… what he did suppose to be good, what to be evil… if thou dost not [share the same mistaken values], how canst thou but be gentle unto him that is in an error?” (p. 62)
- “Get thee presently to their minds… behold what manner of men they be… yet must thou love them still, for by nature they are thy friends.” (p. 83)
- Purpose: perspective‑taking makes patience easier and guides a fitting response.

4) Use your “antidote” virtue
- “For each fault in others, Nature has given us a counteracting virtue; ‘against the unthankful, [she] hath given goodness and meekness, as an antidote.’” (p. 9)
- Purpose: answer vice with the specific counter‑virtue.

### A (dozat)
If I had to reduce Meditations to one practical instruction, it would be this:

— Keep your ruling mind in the present: do the next just, reasonable action, and drop every opinion that says you are harmed by what happens.

Why this is the book’s core, in Marcus’s own words:

- “Cast away from thee opinion, and thou art safe. And what is it that hinders thee from casting of it away?” (p. 113).
- Let the mind “live by herself… doing that which is just, accepting whatsoever doth happen, and speaking the truth always… then shalt thou… pass the remainder of thy days without troubles and distractions” (p. 109).

- Morning: “It is to go about a man’s work that I am stirred up… Was it not… that thou mightest always be busy and in action?” (p. 38). Set one aim: do the next just thing.
- During the day: When disturbed, say “this is only my opinion”—and drop it (p. 113; p. 29–30). Then return to the present task.
- Often: Step back into your “inner citadel” (retire into yourself), keep a few short precepts ready, and recall that outcomes and reputations are outside you; your charge is the justice of your present action (p. 29–30; p. 27).

That single habit—present justice in action, refusal of disturbing opinions, acceptance of what comes—captures Marcus’s whole discipline.

### B (medium fix)
One instruction: Do the present act justly, with your ruling mind in accord with nature; let everything else be as it is.

Why this sums the book
- Marcus keeps returning to “the present act.” “Go about every action as thy last action, free from all vanity, passion, hypocrisy” (p. 18). “There is nobody that can hinder thee … from doing and speaking what is agreeable to the nature whereof thou art a part” (p. 19).
- He defines what the present act must be: “Do nothing … but what reason proceeding from that regal and supreme part shall for the good and benefit of men suggest unto thee” (p. 30–31). And, as a quick test: “If it be not fitting, do it not. If it be not true, speak it not” (p. 111).
- He shows how to keep the mind steady while you do it: see that “all is but opinion” and that “the things … reach not unto the soul; … from opinion only … proceed the tumult and the trouble” (p. 21; p. 29–30).

How to apply it (one concrete pass)
- Pause and examine your first impression: remind yourself “this is only an opinion” until tested (p. 21; p. 29–30).
- Ask two quick questions: Is it fitting? Is it true? (p. 111).
- Choose the deed reason recommends “for the good and benefit of men” (p. 30–31), and do it “as thy last action” (p. 18).
- Accept the outcome as nature’s—indifferent to your worth—and move to the next right act (p. 19–20; p. 48).

Nuance that matters
- This is not passivity. It is strenuous service with inner freedom: “Ever to be careful to attain to the true natural apprehension of every fancy … and ever to do that which is just” (p. 111).
- Be ready to be corrected: act by reason, and “if any man … be able to rectify thee … be always ready to change thy mind … from some probable apparent ground of justice” (p. 30–31).

If you keep only one habit from Meditations, keep this: do the next right thing, here and now, with a steady mind; accept the rest.

### A (dozat)
Marcus turns memento mori into a practical discipline for living now. Remembering death is what keeps the mind on its proper work in the present: to judge rightly, act justly, and accept what comes according to nature.
