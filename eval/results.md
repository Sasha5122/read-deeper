Short answer: Marcus’s “ruling faculty” is the rational, governing part of you (the mind/understanding/“leading principle”) that judges impressions, gives or withholds assent, directs impulses, and so determines your character and actions. For the Stoic Marcus, it is a “spark” of the universal Reason (Logos) in you—your “god within”—and your task is to keep it pure, just, and in harmony with nature.

How he describes it
- He names it “the mistress and overruling part of man; reason,” and warns: “suffer not that excellent part to be brought in subjection … drawn up and down with unreasonable and unsociable lusts” (Book II, XVI; p. 17).
- It is the inner power that can interpret events and remain free: “My understanding, that ruleth over all, will not of itself bring trouble and vexation upon itself” (VII, XIII; p. 60); “Cast away from thee opinion, and thou art safe” (XII, XIX; p. 113).
- It is the “inward mistress part” that can re-direct itself when events change, like a great fire that turns obstacles into fuel (IV, I; p. 28–29).
- He often equates it with a divine presence: “Let thy God that is in thee to rule over thee” (III, V; p. 25); to “live with the Gods” is to show them “a soul … performing whatsoever is pleasing to that Spirit, whom (being part of himself) Jove hath appointed to every man as his overseer and governor” (VII, XXI; p. 65–66).

What it does
- Receives impressions and forms judgments (the “opinative faculty”)—and “in her indeed is all” (III, X; p. 26–27). Hence his drill: separate the event from the opinion about it.
- Issues impulses and actions: “The rational commanding part … alone can stir up and turn itself; so it maketh both itself to be, and everything that happeneth, to appear unto itself, as it will itself” (VI, VII; p. 48).
- Aligns the microcosm (you) with the macrocosm (Nature): as the Introduction puts it, the cosmos has a “divine ruling principle,” and “virtue in the soul must rule man”; our highest good is “to work with God for the common good” (Intro; p. 5–6). Your ruling faculty is the human analogue of that cosmic ruling principle.

How to treat it
- Keep it “unspotted by pleasure, undaunted by pain” (III, IV; p. 24), simple and self-contained (XII, II; p. 109; XII, XV; p. 111).
- Guard it from passions by watching opinions (II, XI; p. 20; III, X; p. 26–27; XII, XIX; p. 113).
- Aim its work at justice and the common good (III, V; p. 25; IV, IV; p. 30).

In sum, the ruling faculty (Greek: hegemonikon) is your rational, sovereign mind—your “inner citadel”—a fragment of the universal Reason. Freedom and virtue lie in keeping it aligned with nature and society, mastering judgments, and refusing to let externals drag it from its proper rule.

Short answer: for Marcus, remembering death is the main lever that turns you back to the only time you ever have—the present—and to the only thing that is yours—your ruling mind and its just action. It strips away fear, fame-seeking, and distraction, so you can do “this” action rightly, now.

How he ties death to the present throughout:

- Death is natural and near; accept it to free the present for virtue.
  - “To die… must among the rest be reckoned as one of the duties and actions of our lives” (VI.2, p. 48).  
  - “Not as though thou hadst thousands of years to live. Death hangs over thee: whilst yet thou livest, whilst thou mayest, be good” (IV.14, p. 31).  
  - Dying is “a work of nature” and therefore not to be feared (II.10–11, p. 20).

- Only the present is yours; death equalizes all lengths of life.
  - “Go about every action as thy last action” (II.2, p. 18–19).  
  - Even if you lived “three thousand… or ten thousands of years,” you can lose only the present moment; the present is “equal unto all men” (II.12, p. 20–21).  
  - “No operation… ceasing… can be truly said to suffer any evil… So… the whole body of our actions (which is our life), if in time it cease… cannot be hurt” (XII.17, p. 112–113).

- Therefore, circumscribe attention to the now and do the next right thing.
  - “Circumscribe the present time… examine whatsoever it be that is happened” (VII.21, p. 62).  
  - “Contract thy whole life to the measure and proportion of one single action” (VIII.30, p. 74).  
  - “What is the present estate of my understanding? For herein lieth all indeed” (XII.26, p. 114–115).

- Death dissolves fame and posterity; so ignore them and act rightly now.
  - Posthumous praise? “What is that to thee?” (VII.16, p. 31–32).  
  - Consider how quickly “all things are dissolved,” and how soon even names are forgotten (II.9, p. 19; IV.27–28, p. 34–35; VII.42, p. 56).

- Memento mori shrinks pettiness and anger, clears space for present justice and kindness.
  - Keep before you “the several deaths of men of all sorts,” so you won’t be “puffed up” or “distracted with cares” (VII.42, p. 56).  
  - When wronged, remember life is “but for a moment of time,” so return to your own present duty (IX.20, p. 84; II.15, p. 17–18; IV.3, p. 29–30).

- The cosmos is perpetual change; seeing death as change roots you in present alignment with nature.
  - “This world is mere change, and this life, opinion” (IV.3, p. 30).  
  - Whether you die “to-morrow, or next day,” it’s “no great matter” compared with living in accord with nature now (IV.38, p. 36).

- The decisive fear is not “ceasing to live,” but “never begin[ning] to live according to nature.”
  - If you can “readily leave all things” when death comes, fearing only that you failed to live rightly, you can possess what matters “even now” (XII.1–2, p. 108–110).  
  - “No man lives properly, but that very instant of time which is now present” (XII.19, p. 113).

In sum: Marcus’s constant rehearsal of death is a practical exercise to prune away past-and-future rumination and the lure of reputation, so that attention returns to the present act of a rational, social being—“whilst yet thou livest, … be good” (IV.14, p. 31).

Not “just tolerate.” Marcus is training you to be unprovokable and then to act justly.

- First, expect friction so you’re not surprised or soured by it. “Betimes in the morning say to thyself, This day I shall have to do with…unsociable [people]” — they act “through ignorance of that which is truly good and bad” (p. 17). Forewarned, you don’t give them your composure.

- Second, reframe them as kin, not enemies: “we are all born to be fellow‑workers… for such therefore to be in opposition is against nature” (p. 17). This forbids hatred, not correction.

- Third, act for the common good without rancor. If you can, “teach [the one who offends] with love and meekness, and…show him his error; but if thou canst not, then…go on” (p. 89). “Do nothing…contrary to the community” (p. 25). This isn’t passivity; it’s disciplined strength. Marcus fought wars and governed; he insists you bear insults, not injustices. The Stoic move is:
- keep your equilibrium,
- correct firmly (set boundaries, give candid feedback, escalate when duty requires),
- continue your work for the common good—without hatred or pettiness (p. 29–30, 25).
